Two machines in this project — do not mix their numbers

The arm is the six-axis steel model in robotic arm.f3d. It is the mechanical design, and every dimension, mass and motor figure in the report describes it.

The worked example is a two-link planar arm, L1 = 250 mm and L2 = 150 mm, used in week 15 only. Full six-axis kinematics cannot be derived by hand in a week, so the maths is developed on a case small enough to solve on paper and check in code, then the method is applied to the real arm's DH table. Its 400 mm reach and 0.5 kg payload describe nothing physical.

Anything belonging to the worked example is fenced in a dashed box like this one. A figure from one must never be presented as describing the other. The reference paper in the folder, Jadhav et al., is a third and different arm again — cite it, do not take numbers from it.

Week 14

Specification and concept

Write down what the machine has to do and why it is shaped the way it is. All paper. The model already exists, so this week is about being able to justify it rather than having chosen it by accident.

MON14.1Paper

Write the specification

Defined the task: lift a part from a conveyor and sort it into one of three bins. Set the specification from the model as built — 857.5 mm reach at full extension, 826.7 mm overall height, a Ø220 mm bolted base footprint, and six revolute axes. Listed the constraints: bolted to a floor plate, mains power, and an electric gripper since no compressed air is available.

TUE14.2Paper

Why six axes, and why articulated

Compared cartesian, SCARA, articulated and delta layouts against reach, footprint, speed and stiffness. An articulated arm gives the largest reach for the smallest floor area, which is what a Ø220 mm base demands. Recorded the cost of that choice honestly: every joint adds a gearbox, a source of backlash, and another mass hanging off the ones below it.

WED14.3Paper

Weighted decision matrix

Scored the four layouts 1–5 against five weighted criteria — reach-to-footprint 30%, cycle time 25%, cost 20%, complexity 15%, stiffness 10%. The articulated arm scored highest, with SCARA second and losing on vertical travel. Noted that six axes is more than a pure pick-and-place needs: three would position the gripper, and the extra three set its orientation.

TUE15.2Paper

Forward and inverse kinematics by hand

Derived the forward map from the link geometry, then inverted it with the cosine rule, taking θ2 first and θ1 second. The ± on the arccos gives two valid solutions for the same point, elbow-up and elbow-down. Chose elbow-up as standard so the forearm stays clear of the conveyor guard.

x = L1·cos θ1 + L2·cos(θ1 + θ2)   cos θ2 = (x² + y² − L1² − L2²) / (2·L1·L2)

WED15.3Python

Code it and round-trip test it

Wrote arm.py, about twenty lines, with a forward and an inverse function. Tested by round-tripping: take joint angles, compute a position, feed that position back in, and check the original angles come out. Caught a sign error in θ1 immediately that Tuesday's hand derivation had hidden. 

# arm.py — worked example, two-link planar
import numpy as np
L1, L2 = 250.0, 150.0

def forward(t1, t2):
    return (L1*np.cos(t1) + L2*np.cos(t1+t2),
            L1*np.sin(t1) + L2*np.sin(t1+t2))

def inverse(x, y, elbow_up=True):
    c2 = (x*x + y*y - L1**2 - L2**2) / (2*L1*L2)
    t2 = np.arccos(np.clip(c2, -1, 1))
    if not elbow_up: t2 = -t2
    t1 = np.arctan2(y, x) - np.arctan2(L2*np.sin(t2), L1 + L2*np.cos(t2))
    return t1, t2

CAD, mass properties and motor sizing

WED16.3Fusion

Interference and range-of-motion check

Drove each joint through its full range watching for collisions between the links and the base column, and recorded the angle at which each one fouls. These become the software limits in week 17 — they are tighter than the purely kinematic limits, and the CAD finds them where the maths never would.

THU16.4Paper

Free body diagram and joint torque

Drew the free body diagram for the worst case, the arm fully extended horizontally at 857.5 mm with the payload gripped. Took the masses from Fusion, 46.855 kg above the shoulder, and bounded the moment rather than computing it exactly: placing every gram at full reach can only overstate it, which gives 411 N·m, or 822 N·m with a safety factor of 2. The bound is rigorous and deliberately pessimistic, since the heavy links sit near the shoulder and only the 0.28 kg fingers reach the end.

T_bound = ( Σm_i + m_payload ) · g · R_max × SF = 822 N·m

FRI16.5Paper

Motor and gearbox selection

Sized the shoulder drive against the 822 N·m bound. A 10 N·m industrial servo needs 82:1; a 3 N·m servo through a harmonic drive needs 274:1. Recorded plainly that this is a bound and not a measurement: the exact figure needs the centres of mass re-read with the arm posed straight out, and sizing against a pessimistic bound buys a heavier drive than the arm strictly needs. Trade-off to check in week 17: a gearbox divides speed in the same ratio it multiplies torque, so a reduction this large costs cycle time.

WED17.3Python

Control architecture and PID

Specified closed-loop position control on each joint independently, and drew the loop: demanded angle, error, controller, drive, motor, link, with the encoder feeding the measured angle back. Simulated one joint as a second-order system and swept the PID gains. Proportional alone left a steady-state error because gravity holds the arm off target; integral removed it and derivative damped the overshoot. Settled slightly over-damped, since overshoot on a loaded arm swings the payload.

Week 18

Safety, integration and hand-in

Show the design is safe and pull the five weeks together. If the project ends at week 17, move Monday and Tuesday into it and drop the rest.

MON18.1Paper

FMEA

Carried out a failure mode and effects analysis across the subsystems, scoring each mode for severity, occurrence and detection and multiplying for a risk priority number. The highest was gripper release on power loss — a steel part dropped from 800 mm is a safety problem, not just a quality one — so specified a normally-closed gripper that holds without power. A gravity-backdriving joint on power loss scored next and needs a fail-safe brake.

TUE18.2Paper

Safety and risk assessment

Wrote the risk assessment: trapping points between the links, crushing at the bins, and unexpected start-up during maintenance. A six-axis arm with an 857.5 mm reach sweeps a large volume, so the guarded envelope is set by that radius plus the payload. Specified a latching emergency stop that removes drive power directly rather than through software, and an interlocked access door.

Drew the full signal and power schematic showing all six drives, the encoders, the limit switches and the e-stop chain. Totalled peak current across the six motors plus the controller and sensors, and sized the supply with headroom for stall current on the shoulder, which dominates.

FRI18.5Paper

Hand-in and reflection

Finalised the report and slides. Reflection: the decision that drove everything downstream was building in steel at this scale, which set the joint torques and therefore the drives, the power budget and the cycle time. If I repeated the project I would take the mass properties out of the CAD before choosing motors rather than after, because sizing and geometry are coupled and I worked them in the wrong order.

Keeping it honest

  • The worked example is fenced everywhere it appears. Its 400 mm reach and 0.5 kg payload describe no physical object. If a marker can point at a figure and ask which arm it belongs to, the fencing has failed.
  • Fill the mass table before sizing anything. Every torque, drive and power figure downstream depends on it, and a steel arm at 857 mm is far heavier than intuition suggests.
  • A cycle time that misses is a result, not a failure. Finding that the gear ratios needed for the torque cost you the target is exactly the coupling the report should discuss.
  • The cited paper is someone else's. Jadhav et al. describe a 3-DOF arm with different link lengths. Reference it for method; take no numbers from it.
  • Four weeks only? Move Monday and Tuesday of week 18 into week 17 in place of the trajectory day, and fold cycle time into the PID day.
